Background
Oregon’s municipalities, as defined by Municipal Audit Law, include all counties, cities, school districts,
special districts, and public corporations subject to control by local governments. Municipal Audit Law
provides instructions for annual reports that municipalities must submit to the Secretary of State.1
Municipal Audit Law requires the Secretary of State to prescribe the minimum standards for the conduct of
each audit. The Secretary of State requires independent accountants to follow professional auditing
standards set by the American Institute of Certified Public Accountants (AICPA) and issue financial reports
in accordance with generally accepted accounting principles.
1,841 municipalities are registered with the Secretary of State; all are subject to annual reporting
requirements, but not all are subject to an audit. The type of report required depends on the municipality
and its expenditures. All counties, school districts, and public charter schools, as well as municipalities with
total annual expenditures of more than $1 million, are required to have their financial statements audited
each year.2 Most municipalities operate on a July 1 to June 30 fiscal year. Audits are performed by local
Certified Public Accountant firms and reports are to be filed with the Secretary of State by December 31 —
six months after the end of the fiscal year. Extensions could be for up to an additional six months, making
due dates as late as June 30 the following year.
Municipalities with total annual expenditures between $250,000 and $1 million are not required to have an
audit and may opt to have agreed-upon procedures (AUP) performed by an independent accountant.
Municipalities with total annual expenditures of $250,000 or less may submit reports in lieu of audit where
they self-report expenditures on forms prescribed by the Secretary of State.3 Financial reports are posted
on the Secretary of State’s website.4
Fiscal Year 2024
Of the 1,841 municipalities in Oregon, 1,204 were expected to file audit reports with the Secretary of State
for fiscal year 2024. However, 114 (9.5%) of the required audits were still not filed as of December 31,
2025. This is an increase from the prior year when 106 municipalities did not file required audit reports (see
Figure 2).

Report Filing Extensions
Municipal Audit Law permits a municipality to request additional time to file its annual audit report for good
cause or due to extraordinary circumstances. The request must include the cause for the delay and the
expected report delivery date. Both the government official and the independent accountant make the
request.

There were fewer requests for extension in fiscal year 2024 compared to the prior year, although still higher
than the number received prior to the COVID-19 pandemic. We received requests from 278 municipalities,
one of every four audit filers.
The reasons cited for extensions vary, including delays from vacancies of key staff at both municipalities
and firms, challenges with accounting software implementation and errors, operational and building
maintenance challenges, and switching audit firms.
Requests for extension are typically denied when a request does not demonstrate good cause as required,
when the request is related to a report in lieu of audit or AUP reporting, or when a municipality has routinely
filed late reports or requested extensions for similar reasons year after year.
The percentage of extension requests approved for the 2024 filing year was 62%, which fell below the prior
year percentage. This is largely due to the number of municipalities on the delinquent filers list and
challenges on getting caught up; extensions are typically denied when the municipality has a history of late
filing.
Audit Deficiencies and Plans of Action
Most local governments receive a “clean” audit report each year without any qualifications or deficiencies
noted. However, when deficiencies are reported, the governing body of a municipality must determine the
corrective measures necessary to address the deficiencies. Municipal Audit Law requires a municipality to
file with the Secretary of State a copy of its plan of action within 30 days after filing its annual report. The
plan must include appropriate actions to address the deficiencies noted and an estimate of the timeframe
necessary to complete the planned actions.
Statutory Requirements — Annual Summary Reporting
Oregon law requires the Secretary of State to prepare an annual summary report, which is to include the
following:5
 A list of municipalities required to file audit reports with the Secretary;
 A list of municipalities that filed audit reports with the Secretary within six months after the close of
the year under audit;
 A list of municipalities that did not request a filing extension and did not file their report within six
months of close of fiscal year;
 A list of municipalities that requested and were granted filing extensions and if they met their filing
extension deadline;
 A list of number and type of deficiencies cited in audit reports; and
 A description of whether the municipality submitted a plan of action to the Secretary of State.
This report and appendices fulfill the requirements outlined in the law.

The requirements in Municipal Audit Law are intended for municipalities to be financially accountable to the
public. Many stakeholders are involved in the municipal audit process. Government officials understand
the annual reporting requirements; independent accountants provide timely audit services; the Secretary
of State works as a resource for government officials and the audit community throughout the year to aid in
understanding and supporting compliance; and the people of Oregon are informed of their local
government’s financial activities.
Audit Report Filings
Of the 1,841 municipalities actively registered in Oregon, 1,204 were expected to file an audit with the
Secretary of State. As shown in Figure 3, 66% of these municipalities filed their audit reports on time or
within their approved extension deadline. Of the 278 that requested and received extensions, 90 filed their
report within the extended deadline.
As of December 31, 2025, 114 municipalities had not filed a fiscal year 2024 audit report; these
municipalities included four counties, 40 cities, two school districts and education service districts (ESDs),
four charter schools, 15 fire districts, and 49 special districts.

Internal Control Deficiencies
At least one significant deficiency or material weakness in internal controls was reported for 324
municipalities. A deficiency exists when the design or operation of controls does not allow management or
employees to prevent, or detect and correct, financial misstatements on a timely basis. Inadequate
segregation of duties and the inability to prepare financial statements are common in smaller governments
with limited staff and remain the most prevalent deficiencies reported.
Accounting Errors and Misstatements
Auditors reported one or more significant deficiencies or material weaknesses related to accounting errors
and misstatements for 150 municipalities. Many could have been detected and corrected timely if the
municipality had a stronger system of internal control. Some common errors are recording amounts in the
wrong account, fund, or reporting period, and misstatements for failing to recognize and record accruals
required for revenues and expenditures. Inaccurate reporting makes financial statements less effective for
decision-making.

Compliance with Laws, Rules, and Regulations
Auditors reported one or more instances of significant noncompliance with laws, rules, regulations, or
grantor requirements for 42 municipalities. Ad valorem taxes, such as property taxes, are a major source of
revenue for most of Oregon’s municipalities. As such, municipalities are required to prepare, adopt, and
execute annual or biennial budgets in accordance with Oregon’s Local Budget Law. Significant
noncompliance with those requirements may result in deficiencies reported by the auditor. Other instances
of noncompliance may pertain to Oregon’s Public Contracting Code, failure to obtain required fidelity bond
insurance coverage, or failing to comply with federal or state grant requirements.
Conclusion
This summary report fulfills the legislative requirements of Oregon’s Municipal Audit Law and presents a
summary of financial reporting activity as of December 31, 2025. We appreciate the ongoing partnership
with independent accountants and Oregon municipalities in ensuring compliance with the law and
enhanced transparency.
We have included the summary information required by law in the following appendices:
Appendix A  is a list of municipalities that filed timely audit reports within the six-month statutory deadline
or within an approved extension deadline.
Appendix B  is a list of municipalities that either filed audit reports beyond an approved extension of time or
filed late and did not file a request for an extension.
Both Appendix A and Appendix B identify whether letters reporting material weaknesses or significant
deficiencies issued by the independent accountants were received and whether a plan of action adopted by
the governing body was filed. Both items are required to be filed with the Secretary of State when
significant or material deficiencies are reported.
Appendices A and B also include the number and type of material weaknesses and significant deficiencies
reported by the independent accountants and filed with the Secretary of State.

ABOUT THE SECRETARY OF STATE AUDITS DIVISION
The Oregon Constitution provides that the Secretary of State shall be, by virtue of the office, Auditor of
Public Accounts. The Audits Division performs this duty. The division reports to the Secretary of State and
is independent of other agencies within the Executive, Legislative, and Judicial branches of Oregon
government. The Secretary of State has constitutional authority to audit all state officers, agencies, boards
and commissions.
